What Can Sensory Overload Feel Like in a Classroom?

Many autistic children and children with ADHD experience sound, light, and touch differently. Some find background input hard to tune out, some notice less of it, and some seek more of it. This simulator shows one possible experience of overload. It can't show what any particular child feels.

01

Harsh Lighting

Lighting that seems fine to one person can be distracting or even painful to another. Older fluorescent tubes flicker 100 to 120 times a second, which has been linked with headaches and eyestrain.

Light
02

Background Noise

A ticking clock, scratching pencils, and a humming fan can be hard to filter out, leaving less attention for the teacher's voice.

Sound
03

Racing Thoughts

ADHD can make it harder to stay on task and easier to be sidetracked. A stray thought can pull attention away just as you are holding a number in mind.

Attention
04

Overload

When everything becomes too much, an autistic person may have a meltdown or withdraw. It isn't bad behavior. The shaking screen here is only a visual metaphor.

Overwhelm

What the Effects Represent

Glare: Lighting can be uncomfortable or distracting for some people. This effect is deliberately slow and never flashes.
Noise: Everyday sounds can be hard to separate from the sound you need to hear.
Racing thoughts: ADHD is linked with distractibility. Dopamine appears to be involved, but researchers are still working out how.
Overload: The meter and its levels are invented for this demonstration. They don't measure anything in a real person.
Hyperfocus: ADHD can also mean getting so absorbed in one thing that everything else fades, even when it isn't what you need to focus on. Research on hyperfocus is still limited. Turn on Hyperfocus and move your pointer over the room.

What Actually Helps

Small changes to the room and the routine can make school more manageable. No single list fits every child, so treat these as starting points. After a quiz, choose Try again with support to test some of them in the simulator.

Ask the child first

Children and families can often say what helps most. Needs differ from person to person and can change over time.

Reduce noise and glare

Limit distractions, allow noise-cancelling headphones or ear defenders, and check that classroom lights aren't flickering.

Make room to move

Build in breaks and time to move around, rather than expecting a child to stay still for a whole lesson.

Take pressure off memory

Offer extra help with staying organized, and use technology or written supports so everything doesn't have to be held in mind.

Allow more time

Extra time on assignments, projects, and tests gives a child a fairer chance to show what they know.

Keep things predictable

Predictable routines can reduce anxiety. If a child becomes overwhelmed, respond to the distress and look for what caused it. A meltdown is not bad behavior.

About This Simulator

This is an awareness tool. It isn't a diagnostic test or medical advice, and your score says nothing about you or anyone else.

Simulations have limits. Research has not found that they reliably improve attitudes toward disabled people, and they can leave people feeling distress or pity. Use this to start a conversation, and listen to autistic people and people with ADHD about their own experiences.
